A proof of concept proves that an idea *can* work. Production proves that it can
be *operated* — safely, affordably, and by a team that isn&apos;t you. The
distance between those two is where most enterprise AI projects stall, and it&apos;s
rarely because the model wasn&apos;t good enough.

## The demo hides the hard part

A POC is optimized to impress in a controlled setting. It runs on clean data, a
friendly question, and a forgiving audience. None of the things that make
production hard are visible in that room:

- **Evaluation.** How do you know it&apos;s still good next month, on data you
  haven&apos;t seen?
- **Monitoring.** How do you detect drift before your users do?
- **Security & governance.** Who can see what, and how is every decision logged?
- **Cost at scale.** What does this cost at 100× the demo&apos;s volume?
- **Human-in-the-loop.** Where does a person stay in control of consequential
  actions?

## Make the gap visible

The most useful thing I do at this stage is refuse to let &ldquo;almost
done&rdquo; stay vague. I turn the remaining work into an explicit checklist with
the customer, so the last stretch is planned rather than assumed. A POC with a
90%-impressive demo is often less than halfway to production — and naming that
honestly builds more trust than optimism does.

## Treat AI like the production software it is

Non-deterministic systems need *more* operational rigor than deterministic ones,
not less. Evaluation sets, monitoring, audit logging, and clear failure modes
aren&apos;t bureaucracy — they&apos;re what let you put the thing in front of real
customers and sleep at night. [ResolveIQ](/projects/resolveiq) was built
specifically to show that gap end to end.

The goal was never a great demo. It was a system a team can confidently own after
I&apos;m gone.
